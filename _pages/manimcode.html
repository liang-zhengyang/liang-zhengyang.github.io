Abstract

Manim supports precise, programmatic video generation, but a successfully rendered educational animation can still have visual defects, missing explanations, or incorrect content. Rendering does not directly identify the code changes needed to correct these visual failures, so feedback-driven revision is needed. Existing agentic frameworks use iterative rendering and revision but typically rely on large models, often proprietary. Existing Manim datasets primarily contain final programs and often lack quality verification and the trajectories of feedback and revisions needed to learn this refinement process.

We introduce ManimData and ManimCode, a data and training framework for feedback-driven educational animation generation. We construct ManimData through a render-guided pipeline that iteratively generates, executes, reviews, and revises Manim programs with retrieved API documentation. Its 24,034 render-verified, quality-filtered records preserve intermediate programs, execution errors, visual critiques, and corrective revisions. We use role-conditioned supervised fine-tuning on these trajectories to train ManimCode, a single 4B model, to write, review, and revise animation programs. To improve correction of errors that differ from those in the collected trajectories, we then train the Reviser with reinforcement learning on self-generated states, using rendering outcomes and reference-free quality feedback.

ManimCode achieves 96.6% and 97.0% render success on ManimData-test and ManimBench, compared with 52.6% and 82.0% for the untrained 4B model under the same inference setup, while also improving video quality scores from 2.48 to 2.94 and from 3.00 to 3.44. Integrating ManimCode into two long-form educational video generation systems increases completed videos from 19/117 to 107/117 and from 53/239 to 239/239, alongside improvements in educational effectiveness and visual quality.

ManimData

ManimData pairs each request with a render-verified program, its video, and the trajectory of errors, critiques, and revisions that produced the program. Inputs come from two sources: requests derived from Wikipedia STEM topics, and public community programs from seven Hugging Face datasets, JanusCode-800K, and the ManimBench training split.

Every input enters the same render–review–revise loop. Qwen3.8-27B writes or revises the program with up to 10 renders per input, looking up APIs in a knowledge base of 2,219 cards that covers ManimCE v0.19.1 and eight domain plugins. A layout checker reports objects outside the frame, overlapping text, and labels too small to read, and the Reviewer scores eight sampled frames on request alignment, content completeness, visual clarity, and animation quality. An input is admitted only if its best version reaches a mean score of at least 3.75.

Method

Role-conditioned trajectory supervision

Each trajectory supervises the writing, reviewing, and revision steps of one Qwen3.5-4B model, conditioned on its role. The Writer targets the record's final program, so it learns from the refinements made during construction. The Reviewer targets the recorded scores and critique. The Reviser targets complete revised programs from two kinds of successful transitions: execution repairs that turn a crash into a render, and quality revisions that raise the mean review score. We also supervise the retrieval queries so the model can look up API cards at inference.

Revision RL on self-generated states

A stronger model collected the trajectories, so they may not capture the mistakes of the 4B model. We therefore let the supervised model write and review its own drafts on requests disjoint from the evaluation sets. Each crash becomes a repair state and each unsatisfactory review a critique state. The Reviser samples eight candidate revisions per state, and we render them all.

On a repair state, a candidate that fails to render earns zero, and one that renders earns a reward from Qwen3.8-27B's request alignment and content completeness scores. On a critique state, a successful candidate is compared with the input program in both presentation orders: it earns +1 if it wins both, −1 if it loses both, and 0 otherwise, while a candidate that fails to render earns −1. Near-duplicates earn zero. No reference program or target video is needed. We optimize with leave-one-out policy optimization (RLOO), using a clipped policy-gradient objective and a KL penalty toward the supervised policy.

Inference

A single 4B model runs the same render–review–revise loop used during data construction, without the larger model. Each request starts from a newly generated program, and the loop retains the best successfully rendered candidate within the budget.

Results

Scene-level generation

With a 4B generator, ManimCode reaches 96.6% and 97.0% render success on ManimData-test and ManimBench, ahead of the evaluated general-purpose models (at most 62.4% and 83.0%) and released Manim-specialized systems (at most 67.0% and 73.0%). Its video quality is comparable to GPT-4o and approaches recent mixture-of-experts models, particularly on ManimBench. The 4B rows separate the components: the loop alone lifts render success with only modest changes in video quality, trajectory SFT improves both, and Reviser RL mainly adds render success (8.0 and 5.0 points).

How far to trust the judge

One member of our team, blind to which system produced which video, compared 98 pairs from ManimCode and GPT-4o or Qwen3.8-27B. For VQ gaps of at least 0.5, the annotator agreed with the judge on 79% of decided pairs, against 29% for gaps below 0.25, so small score differences should be read with care. At the system level, human preferences align with the judge's rankings in three of four comparisons, favoring ManimCode over GPT-4o overall but Qwen3.8-27B on ManimData-test.
